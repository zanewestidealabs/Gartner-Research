Outcome-Based Pricing Fails Tech CEOs in
Services
24 February 2026- ID G00846386- 9 min read
By: Don Ribar
Initiatives: Technologies and Markets
Outcome-based pricing is a trending term and sounds appealing,
but hidden complexity and costs often make it impractical. Tech
CEOs should instead use transparent models using components
familiar to buyers that offer more flexibility, protect revenue and
balance risk better.
Overview
Key Findings
■ Despite a perception that outcome-based pricing is in common usage, the reality is
that only 19% of services buyers are using these types of arrangements and on the
seller side, only 13% of services agreements use outcome-based attributes. 1 ,2
■ Buyers prefer providers to take on more risk, but often don’t want to pay more for
that benefit. Defining and measuring success in outcome-based models is complex,
time-consuming, expensive, and can strain strong relationships. Tech CEOs and their
customers are often unprepared and unwilling to incur these overheads and costs.
■ The additional pricing complexity can extend sales cycles, strain customer
procurement practices and procedures and introduce contracting risk and volatility
to customer budgeting and provider revenue planning.

Recommendations
■ Implement a transparent pricing approach that utilizes a “best-of-breed”
combination of subscription, fixed, consumption-based, and bonus fee components
tied to specific objectives and performance thresholds. Side-stepping outcome-
based pricing does not mean avoiding focusing on the identification and delivery of
clear business results.
■ Meet customers’ desire for simplicity by using components with which they and
their procurement officers are familiar in a way that demonstrates what they are
paying for and when. Lowering the perceived risk of working with you and showing
how you are taking on more risk reinforces buyer preferences for business partners
and not just vendors.
■ Define expected results upfront, automate data capture for select and limited
metrics to provide real-time performance monitoring of actuals against
expectations, to adjust activities if needed and to support success fees without
elaborate administration.
Strategic Planning Assumption(s)
Through 2031, less than 25% of tech CEO services contracts will utilize outcome-based
pricing.
Analysis
At first glance, the idea of outcome-based pricing sounds incredibly attractive. Your
customer is charged and pays you when you achieve an outcome, for example, if you
build and implement an agentic solution that automatically resolves certain classes of
incidents, then they pay you per resolution, or bundle of resolutions. In the provider view,
you are taking a significant risk for successful delivery and should be compensated
accordingly. However, reality does not make this practical in many situations. Tech CEOs
must evaluate how they can tailor their pricing approach to meet the customer’s need for
value and their own requirements to sell and deliver profitable work with acceptable risk.
The Assumed Advantages and Realities of Outcome-Based Pricing
In practice today, these models are not used extensively by tech CEOs as a primary
pricing mechanism. Broad use of these models remains elusive.
The single biggest reason for limited usage of outcome-based models is the difficulty and
complexity in defining and measuring success.

Customers Want Outcomes and Shared Risk — But Not the Complexity
Regardless of the billing method, customers demand tangible business results. The 2025
Gartner Digital Services Buyer Behavior Survey confirms that a majority of organizations
now include “fees-at-risk” components in over 20% of service agreements, reflecting a
clear desire for providers to share the financial risk.
Yet outcome-based pricing models still fail tech CEOs for three main reasons:
■ Difficulty in obtaining agreement on the definition of outcomes
■ The effort and cost associated with measurement and attribution
■ The implied variability in customer budgeting and spending (which has provider
implications as well)
The term outcome-based pricing is falling into casual use, typically without context. Tech
CEOs and their sales teams must be mindful to avoid inadvertently confusing customers
about outcomes (which customers always want) versus outcome-based pricing (which
may or may not be in play). Be mindful of the terminology when speaking to customers, in
presentations, proposals, and sales collateral.
Delivering outcomes is mandatory, but charging on that basis for them is difficult and risky
for both providers, customers, and their relationship.
Likewise, accepting more responsibility and putting a portion of provider fees at risk, does
not require an outcome-based model.
Use an Alternative That Is Composable, Transparent, and Bonus-Based
A “composable” approach means breaking down the effort and using components the
customer (and their procurement department) is already familiar with and uses. These
parts can be assembled or reassembled when necessary to suit customer needs or
market shifts. This approach is effectively a hybrid of existing models using a “best-of-
breed” combination of pricing methods to address the delivery of outcomes. The
components can include:
■ Provider’s acceptance of risk and associated bonus or success fees

■ Use of subscription components, including human time, software (traditional/AI),
vendor assets, managed services bundles, and more
■ Recognition that some pricing may be consumption-driven
■ Fixing project fees for discrete or one-time scope

the customer pays for, when and why. It avoids regular use of hourly rates and the
mystery behind bundling too many services into a single large quote. Figure 1 shows a

■ Fixed delivery: One-time costs for design, setup, and implementation.
■ Usage-based fees: Variable costs driven by usage, such as cloud service, API calls,
token usage, overages, and more.
■ Success fee: Provider bonuses or penalties based on the achievement of major
engagement objectives.
■ Core measures: Automated, system-level data collection embedded into the service
offering that proves high level status on usage and data. Limited scope without
excessive administration.
Ultimately, using a composable and flexible approach offers services to tech CEOs,
providing the benefit of protecting their own revenue and enhancing their position with
customers by:
■ Demystifying what the customer is paying for, when and why.
■ Putting a portion of their own fees at risk with clear rewards and penalties.
■ Setting a spending expectation for customer budgeting.
■ Avoiding excessive variability.
■ Reducing the potential for disputes or the perception that value is being siphoned
off to the providers.
■ Better balancing risk.

Evidence
1 2025 Gartner Digital Services Buyer Behavior Survey. This survey sought to
understand the behavior of services buyers in the current changing technology landscape
and economic climate. Respondents answered questions on spending plans related to AI
services and digital product engineering, geopolitical changes and barriers to achieving
digital ambitions. The survey was conducted online from July through August 2025. In
total, 980 respondents participated, from organizations with annual revenue of at least
$50 million or equivalent from Asia/Pacific (25%), North America (38%), Western Europe
(28%), and Southern Europe (9%). Industries represented included banking and securities,
manufacturing, natural resources, communications, media and services, government,
retail, wholesale trade, insurance, utilities, transportation, healthcare providers, and
education. Qualified respondents were decision makers or decision influencers at the
director level or higher, involved in the selection or evaluation of at least one of the
following consulting or outsourcing services within the past two years: business
consulting, technology consulting, application implementation, application managed
services, infrastructure implementation, infrastructure managed services, infrastructure
as a service, business process services, digital product engineering, and marketing,
experience, content and image services. Disclaimer: The results of this survey do not
represent global findings or the market as a whole, but reflect the sentiments of the
respondents and companies surveyed.
