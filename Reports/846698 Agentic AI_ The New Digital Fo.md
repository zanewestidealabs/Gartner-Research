Title (Required)
Agentic AI: The New Digital Forensics Workhorse
Summary (Required)
Agentic AI will dramatically enhance digital forensic enabling faster, more comprehensive, deeper, and
trustworthy incident response capabilities. In order to bring AI capabilities to digital forensics, product leaders
must address digital chain of custody, transparency, and audit requirements.
Executive Summary (Required)
Key Findings (Required)
● Agentic AI can streamline forensic workflows without sacrificing trust. Product leaders who address
“The Real” digital chain of custody requirements through systemic process and verifiable
outcomes, will be in a position to develop “adoptable” industry leading Digital Forensics and
Incident Response capabilities.
● Incident response providers use agents to provide detailed analysis results at machine speed. By
documenting every automated step and model interaction, they ensure that the resulting evidence
meets the strict Daubert and Federal Rule 901 standards for court admissibility.
● Specialized forensic analysis AI models are providing high degree of "procedural rigor.", and 85+%
accuracy rate of source attribution.
Recommendations (Required)
● Prioritize process driven Digital CoC (Chain of Custody), auditability, verification and explainability.
Focus processes and tools offering "explainable" workflows and human-readable rationale through
best practices and frameworks like SHAP or LIME.
● Transition from "what" happened to "how" the evidence is collected through the adoption of Digital
Forensic Knowledge Graphs (DFKGs) enabling AI to visualize complex linkages and timelines.

Analysis (Required)
We have in recent years seen a significant increase in the use of automation and Agentic AI by
threat actors, in particular Nation State affiliated Threat actor groups, to case a significantly wider
net. This has helped with the speed at which Zero Day vulnerabilities have gone from identification
to being weaponized and exploited, in many cases what used to be months is now hours.
However on the defensive side the adoption of Agentic AI is lagging significantly, partially because
of the regulatory and legislative requirements and partially tradition.
Background and Context (Optional)
Digital forensics and incident response (DFIR) has been somewhat lagging behind other areas of
Cybersecurity services in the adoption of AI in a meaningful manner that provides real value to the end
client. There is a critical path in each incident that requires speed and precision requiring a highly specialized
skill set. There are many practitioners that feel the risk associated with the AI “black-box” far outways the
benefits of AI adoption. As a result the more traditional vendors, who have been in this space for longer, and
the larger service providers, have tended to focus on the use of agentic AI for the more process driven and
report writing elements of their services. Although this does provide value, and does save some time it is not
necessarily what should be considered within the critical path of an investigation. The primary focus should
always be minimizing risk and time to recovery. Agentic AI has come a long way in a very short amount of
time and will continue to do so.
For DFIR vendors to remain relevant, product leaders will need to focus the development of their capabilities
on taking advantage of AI where it will be able to have the greatest impact on the investigation, forensic
analysis process. Agentic AI is at the point where it can handle the far more complex tasks at scale,
precision and with extreme consistency 80% to 90% of the time, that is simply not possible when done

DFIR is generally structured into 5 different areas:
● Planning and Preparation - A key component to risk limitation and mitigation is proper
preparation for the inevitable incidents. The objective is to plan for who will take what action
during the incident and to test this on a regular basis.
● Program and Incident Management - Focuses on the structure execution of the plan when an
incident does occur in alignment with the plan. A big part of this is the effective communication
at a regular cadence both internally and externally throughout the incident along with post
incident activities.
● Forensic Analysis and Investigation - The largest part of the incident is always understanding
the nature of the incident and how far and wide it reaches, along with the business impact.
Generally this requires a very senior skill set and is one of the most time sensitive elements of
an incident.
● Containment, Recovery and Remediation - In many cases, in particular with a major incident,
this portion takes the most time but has a significant dependency on the effectiveness and
speed at which the investigation happens.
● Legal and Compliance - Consider this the oversight over the entire incident management
program, from the preparation for an incident, conduct during the incident, to the engagement
with law enforcement and potential litigation implications both from a legislative oversight
perspective and potential civil liability.
Each of these 5 pillars and their sub components have traditionally been very manual and labour intensive
with some level of automation or use of tools being leveraged for certain aspects. Traditionally a lot of the
focus for Product leaders and service providers has been a tech stack that aligns fairly closely to the data
collection used by the Security Operations Center (SOC) for threat monitoring and various others to help with
very specific elements of the investigation depending on its nature, with a heavy reliance on the technical
expertise of individuals to be effective.
There has been some level of Agentic AI adopted to assist with more of the Softer elements of the Incident
management program, such as planning and preparation and Program Management and to a lesser extent
the Legal and Compliance elements. But the highly technical areas have been largely ignored or where there
is some evidence of AI adoption it has once again been focused around report writing, and there has been
some level of automation more aligned with the use of a Security Orchestration and Automated Response
(SOAR). But this is the area where Agentic AI will have the greatest impact.

Conclusion (Optional)
Digital Forensics and Incident Response (DFIR) is lagging behind the rest of the cybersecurity
services industry in adopting Agentic AI or AI in general to enhance delivery capabilities. This is in
part legacy or tradition and in part it is legislation that is lagging which is forcing the manual
approach to the critical path functions within an incident. We have seen some recent movements in
legislation to provide frameworks and guidelines on how evidence should be collected in order to be
admissible, making it extremely difficult.
Threat actors have rapidly moved towards the adoption of large scale automation and AI for their
attack approach with no regard to regulations and without any constraints which has led to a divide
in both the Preemptive and response capabilities and the rapidly evolving threat actor capabilities.
The largest obstacle with the current state of agentic AI in the field is not the capabilities but rather
trust, with concerns about the AI "Black Box”. We are seeing startups take more risks and push the
boundaries of agentic AI in forensic investigation focusing more on the Forensic Analysis &
Investigation, and Response & Remediation portions and less on the process, reporting and
administrative side, which is where the traditional larger vendors have ventured. This is giving
startups an advantage.

Through some emerging technologies like ForensicLLM, which is still largely a research model,
there is a proven approach to the use of an LLM that can achieve a very high degree of accuracy in
evidentiary attribution at a scale and speed that is simply not possible by an individual or even
teams. In order to overcome the trust issues, which is one of the largest barriers to the broader
adoption of AI for Forensic Investigation, these models need to evolve and need to be adopted
more broadly. Standards and legislative allowances for Digital CoC that align the timeline with the
artifacts with the methodology used by the AI, will go a long way to bridging this divide.
It is important to note that the use of AI will not eliminate the need for the specialized investigator
resources, it just shifts their focus from the large scale mundane tasks of grinding through
enormous amounts of data, and detailed report writing to more of a focussed validation and
understanding of the actionable evidence. Ensuring that the evidence and timelines are accurate
and using the tools answer more complex questions that will assist in the training of the models
long term. Forensic Investigation specialists are key to the continued enhancement and training of
the models.
I would expect that there is going to be some level of government oversight and validation of
platforms and tools to meet the strict standards (Daubert and Federal Rule 901 standards) required
by the legal system. There is also likely to be a level of validation by cyber insurance companies as
they look to mitigate their risk. We have seen for years that insurance companies have been
selecting approved IR service providers that meet their standards on their ability to deliver, this will
too evolve into a level of validation of AI LLM’s that are being leveraged for the investigation
process.
The rate at which governance and legislative standards evolve and are adopted is going to need to
be rapid, it has to be. It is important that there are defined standards that are clearly adhered to,
which will need constant human validation but this does not detract from the value that AI can and
will provide, as the solutions and services pivot from the “what has happened” to the “how” the
evidence was collected through the use of Digital Forensic Knowledge Graphs (DFKGs), giving AI
the ability to visualize the timelines and linkages between artifacts and evidence that would not
ordinarily and easily be evident, driving a high degree of accuracy in the evidentiary attribution.
The immediate future is going to see the need for the tandem operation where AI and human
investigators work together to speed up the investigation and get to the point of containment,
recovery and remediation which is the critical path for a client in the midst of a cyber incident.

meaning they can point to the specific file, path, or log entry that supports every claim they
make.
● The emergence of Digital Forensic Knowledge Graphs (DFKGs) allows AI to visualize complex
linkages and timelines. This shifts the focus from "what" happened to "how" the evidence was
found, enabling human analysts to verify the logic behind an agent's reasoning in real time.
● Top-tier incident response firms are now using these agents to provide "incident-response-level"
results at machine speed. By documenting every automated step and model interaction, they
ensure that the resulting evidence meets the strict Daubert and Federal Rule 901 standards for
court admissibility.

a. Incident response leaders should prioritize tools that offer "explainable" workflows. Look for
systems that use frameworks like SHAP or LIME to provide a human-readable rationale for
every investigative decision.
b. Implement a "tandem operation" model where AI agents handle the massive scale of data
ingestion and initial correlation, while human experts focus on validating the acquisition
methodology and the final strategic interpretation.
c. Forensic teams must require that all AI-generated reports include a detailed evidence lineage.
This should consist of deterministic unique identifiers (UIDs) and a record of the specific system
locations (memory, disk, or network) where each artifact was discovered.
d. Ensure your AI infrastructure supports "machine-inclusive" chain of custody. Every action taken
by an agent including model versions, prompts, and tool invocations must be timestamped and
logged in an immutable audit trail to withstand legal scrutiny.
